\begin{abstract}
CubeSats have revolutionized access to space by providing a low-cost, standardized platform for scientific research, technology demonstration, and educational missions. As CubeSat constellations grow, the demand for dedicated and rideshare launch services has increased. This paper provides an overview of existing CubeSat launch vehicles, ranging from dedicated small launchers to rideshare opportunities on medium-lift rockets. We discuss technical considerations such as orbit selection, integration interfaces, and deployment mechanisms. Finally, we highlight emerging trends including electric propulsion, in-space transportation, and the potential of fully reusable small launchers to further reduce barriers to CubeSat access.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{CubeSat, launcher, rideshare, small launch vehicle, space access}

\section{Introduction}
The CubeSat standard, introduced in 1999 by California Polytechnic State University and Stanford University, defines a modular nanosatellite architecture based on 10 cm cubic units (1U) \cite{cubesat_standard}. Over two decades, thousands of CubeSats have been launched, enabling a wide range of missions from Earth observation to interplanetary technology demonstrators. Despite their advantages, CubeSats remain dependent on launch opportunities to reach orbit. Early CubeSats primarily launched as secondary payloads on large rockets, often facing significant delays and limited orbit choice. The rise of dedicated small launch vehicles and improved rideshare coordination has expanded access, yet challenges remain in cost, schedule, and orbital precision.

This paper surveys the current landscape of CubeSat launchers. Section \ref{sec:tech} examines dedicated small launchers and rideshare options. Section \ref{sec:discussion} discusses technical and programmatic considerations. Section \ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with future outlook.

\section{Launch Options for CubeSats}
\label{sec:tech}

\subsection{Dedicated Small Launch Vehicles}
Several launch providers offer vehicles specifically designed for the small satellite market, including CubeSat constellations. Examples include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rocket Lab Electron}: A two-stage liquid-fueled rocket capable of delivering payloads to low Earth orbit (LEO) and sun-synchronous orbit (SSO). Electron has launched numerous CubeSat missions via rideshare and dedicated flights \cite{electron}.
    \item \textbf{Virgin Orbit LauncherOne}: An air-launched liquid rocket deployed from a carrier aircraft, providing flexibility in launch inclination \cite{launcherone}. (Note: Virgin Orbit ceased operations in 2023, but its technology informs future air-launch concepts.)
    \item \textbf{Astra Rocket 3}: A liquid-fueled two-stage rocket designed for rapid, low-cost access to LEO \cite{astra}.
    \item \textbf{Firefly Alpha}: A two-stage liquid-fueled vehicle targeting LEO and SSO missions for small satellites \cite{firefly}.
\end{itemize}
These vehicles often provide options for CubeSat dispensers or custom adapters to accommodate multiple 6U or 12U CubeSats per launch.

\subsection{Rideshare Opportunities on Medium-Lift Rockets}
Many CubeSats continue to access orbit via rideshare on larger launch vehicles. Notable programs include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SpaceX Falcon 9 rideshare}: Regularly scheduled missions (e.g., Transporter series) that carry dozens of small satellites, including CubeSats, to SSO and LEO \cite{transporter}.
    \item \textbf{United Launch Alliance Atlas V}: Offers ESPA (Evolved Expendable Launch Vehicle Secondary Payload Adapter) rings and CubeSat dispensers for secondary payloads \cite{atlasv}.
    \item \textbf{Northrop Grumman Antares}: Primarily supports Cygnus resupply missions but has flown CubeSats as secondary payloads via NanoRacks deployers \cite{antares}.
\end{itemize}
Rideshare offers cost savings but often involves less control over exact orbit and longer manifest timelines.

\subsection{Launch Integration and Deployment}
CubeSats are typically integrated using standardized dispensers such as the Poly-PicoSatellite Orbital Deployer (P-POD) or newer variants like the CubeSat Deployer (CSD) and EXOpod \cite{ppod}. These devices enforce the CubeSat form factor and provide separation springs to impart relative velocity upon deployment. Proper vibration analysis, outgassing control, and debris mitigation practices are required for safe integration.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Orbital Considerations}
The choice of launch vehicle directly affects accessible orbits. Dedicated small launchers often provide tailored inclinations and altitudes, whereas rideshare missions are limited to the primary payload's orbit. For constellations requiring specific planes, multiple launches or onboard propulsion may be necessary.

\subsection{Cost and Access}
Dedicated small launcher prices range from several million to tens of millions of USD per launch, depending on mass and orbit. Rideshare can reduce costs to thousands of USD per CubeSat unit but may involve wait times of months to years. Emerging trends aim to lower costs further through reusability (e.g., SpaceX's Falcon 9 first-stage recovery, Rocket Lab's Electron first-stage reuse efforts) and mass production.

\subsection{Future Trends}
Emerging technologies promise to reshape CubeSat launch:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{In-space Transportation}: Orbital transfer vehicles (OTVs) capable of delivering CubeSats from a common parking orbit to diverse destinations are under development (e.g., Spaceflight Sherpa, Momentus Vigoride) \cite{otv}.
    \item \textbf{Electric Propulsion}: Some CubeSats now include efficient electric thrusters (e.g., Hall effect, ion) enabling post-launch orbit adjustment and reducing launcher performance requirements \cite{electric_prop}.
    \item \textbf{Fully Reusable Small Launchers}: Concepts such as fully reusable two-stage vehicles could dramatically lower launch costs and increase flight rates, benefiting CubeSat constellations \cite{reusable}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Access to space for CubeSats has improved significantly with the advent of dedicated small launch vehicles and optimized rideshare opportunities. While challenges remain in cost, schedule, and orbital flexibility, ongoing innovation in launch services, on-orbit transportation, and propulsion continues to expand possibilities. As launch providers refine their offerings and new entrants increase competition, CubeSat developers can expect greater choice and affordability in achieving their mission objectives.
